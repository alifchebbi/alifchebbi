## 🧫 About me

I am a Tunisian microbiologist whose work sits at the crossroads of microbial ecology, metagenomics and bioinformatics. Over more than a decade of research in Tunisia, the United Kingdom, France and Italy, I have moved from the culture flask to the sequencer and the computer, studying microbes in polluted soils, industrial wastewater, caves, ancient artworks and, more recently, the human gut.

## 🛢️ From oil-eating bacteria to microbial communities

I started with medical biotechnology at the University of Monastir, then a research master's in environmental biotechnology at the University of Sfax. For my PhD in biological engineering (National School of Engineers of Sfax and Centre of Biotechnology of Sfax), I went looking for bacteria able to live on what most organisms cannot: crude oil, used motor oil and the foul-smelling sulfur compounds released by the phosphate industry. Many of these microbes produced **biosurfactants**, natural soap-like molecules, and studying them became the first chapter of my scientific career [1]. In 2014 I spent time at the Mediterranean Institute of Oceanography in Marseille, France, learning culture methods for anaerobic bacteria.

### 🇬🇧 A turning point in Northern Ireland

An important step came in the summer of 2015, when I joined the biosurfactant group of Prof. Ibrahim Banat at the School of Biomedical Sciences of **Ulster University** in Coleraine, Northern Ireland. There I learned the physicochemical characterization of biosurfactants and tested their ability to prevent biofilms, from protecting metal surfaces against biofouling to fighting oral pathogens [2, 3]. The stay connected me to an international research network and introduced me to the non-pathogenic bacterium *Burkholderia thailandensis* as a rhamnolipid producer, a topic I later developed in Italy. Back in Tunisia, a diploma in bioinformatics and genome analysis at the Pasteur Institute of Tunis (2017) opened the door to the computational side of biology.

## 🇮🇹 The Italian years

In 2018 I moved to Italy. As a postdoctoral researcher at the **University of Milano-Bicocca** and then the **University of Brescia**, I worked on turning winery and olive-mill waste into rhamnolipids [4] and on cleaning oil-polluted soils with safe microorganisms [5], in line with the idea of a circular economy.

From 2022 to 2024 I was **Assistant Professor of Microbiology at Roma Tre University**. There my curiosity went underground: I took part in exploring the microbial life of the urban caves beneath Rome and of ancient frescoes, using metagenomics and 16S amplicon sequencing [6, 7]. I was also in charge of teaching **Microbiomics** in the master's program in Molecular, Cellular and Health Biology, where students learned to read microbial communities through DNA and code.

## 🇹🇳 Return to Tunisia

In October 2024 I returned to Tunisia to help build microbiome research capacity in my home country. At ISBST I teach microbiology, molecular microbiology, functional genomics and probiotics, supervise undergraduate research projects, and coordinated the accreditation project for a new bachelor's degree in bioinformatics.

My research now focuses on the gut microbiome in **endometriosis**, host-microbiome interactions and **antimicrobial resistance**, within a **One Health** framework, with a growing use of machine learning to interpret microbiome data. In 2026, with Mohamed Jemaà, I published a review on how gut bacteria may drive chromosomal instability in colorectal cancer [8].

## 🔬 Current project: the gut microbiome in endometriosis

Endometriosis affects about one in ten women of reproductive age, yet it often takes years to diagnose. I lead a nationally funded project (**PEJC 2025**, Tunisian Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research, 2026-2028) asking: **what role does the gut microbiome play in endometriosis?** The project aims to:

- characterize the gut microbiome of women with and without endometriosis using **shotgun functional metagenomics**, looking beyond "who is there" to "what they are doing" (e.g. estrogen metabolism and inflammation pathways);
- use **artificial intelligence** and explainable machine learning to find microbial signatures that could support earlier, non-invasive diagnosis;
- build local capacity in Tunisia for clinical microbiome research, from sampling and sequencing to data analysis.

## 🤝 Open to collaboration

I welcome collaborations on projects dealing with **human health**, especially those relying on **metagenomics** and microbiome data. I can contribute study design, 16S and shotgun sequencing strategies, bioinformatic pipelines, statistical and machine-learning analysis, and training for students and young researchers. Researchers, clinicians and students working on the microbiome in women's health, cancer, infection or antimicrobial resistance are warmly invited to get in touch.
